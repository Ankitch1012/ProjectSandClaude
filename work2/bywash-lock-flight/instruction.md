# Bywash cannot be trusted to plan a passage up the Marsden flight

I volunteer on the Marsden flight and we were given Bywash to plan a passage
before we work a boat up it. You write down the steps you mean to take, and it
tells you which of them can actually be worked, what each one does to the water,
and how the pounds stand afterwards.

It is wrong about nearly all of it, and at a smaller window it stops being
readable at all. Please make it tell the truth.

## What the flight is and what the book says about it

The flight runs from the tail water at the bottom up to the summit pound at the
top. Between each pair of chambers there is a pound, except in the middle, where
two chambers were built back to back as a staircase and share the wall between
them: what lies above the lower chamber of that pair is the upper chamber
itself.

Each chamber has a head gate at its top and a tail gate at its bottom, and a
paddle at each end, and each chamber's own gates and paddles are worked and
written down against that chamber — including where the two chambers of the
staircase meet, which each of them keeps its own account of. A gate may be
opened only when the water each side of it is standing at exactly the same
level, and only when both paddles on that chamber are down. A paddle may be raised only when both gates on that chamber are shut,
only when the chamber has room for the water or water to give, and — where the
paddle draws across a shared wall — only when the chamber on the other side of
that wall is ready and shut up.

Raising the head paddle draws one lockful in from the water above and brings the
chamber up to its full rise. Raising the tail paddle sends one lockful out into
the water below and empties the chamber.

A lockful is the chamber's plan area multiplied by its rise. The gauge book
keeps areas in square feet and rises in feet and inches, and a lockful is
reported in cubic feet.

Drawing a lockful out of a pound lowers that pound by the volume spread over its
surface; sending one back raises it by the same. Every gauged pound may be drawn
down to its permitted depth, and standing at exactly that depth is still
permitted — it is only past it that the flight has to be closed. The tail water
runs away to the river and is not gauged at all.

Water crossing the wall a staircase pair shares is charged to no pound, because
it never leaves the pair. And everything the pounds have given up has to be
somewhere: back in a pound, or standing in a chamber.

A chamber counts as worked through only once the boat is out of it. And the plan
is checked in the order it was written: the first step that cannot be worked is
refused, and nothing after it is worked at all.

## What it actually tells me

The gauge book has every lockful far larger than the chamber could possibly
hold, and the pounds follow it — where a pound is charged at all, a single fill
reads as having drawn it many times past the depth it is allowed to give all
day, which is nonsense for chambers that size.

The water also comes out of the wrong places. Filling a chamber is charged
against the pound below it rather than the one above, so working up the hill
leaves the summit untouched and the pound underneath drained. Lifting the bottom
lock is charged to nothing at all, because the water below it is the ungauged
tail. And the staircase is worse still: the crossing between the two chambers,
which never leaves the pair, is charged to the pound that feeds them, while
filling the upper chamber costs nothing on the books.

Nothing sent back into a pound is ever put to its credit. A lockful run down the
hill leaves the pound above lower and the pound below no higher, and the account
at the foot of the page will not come out however I work it.

The interlocks are not holding either. The gate between the two staircase
chambers will open with a head of water standing on it. A paddle will wind up
with the gate at the other end of the chamber standing wide open. And a gate
will open with a paddle still up.

When a step cannot be worked, Bywash carries on working the rest of the plan
anyway, and the refusal it puts at the top of the plan is the last one it found
rather than the first. And it marks a chamber as worked through the moment its
head gate is opened, before the boat has been anywhere near it, so the count of
chambers passed is wrong from the first lock.

## What it looks like in a smaller window

At about nine hundred pixels wide the elevation stops being a picture of the
flight. The chambers collapse into one another and spill out past the frame they
are drawn in, their names disappear altogether, and the plan comes away from its
column and sits on top of the whole left half of the diagram. The water account
is pinned across the foot of the window over the panels underneath it.

The elevation is the thing I am looking at, so at that width it should have the
room, with the plan and the account below it where I can read them.
